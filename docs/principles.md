# Core principles

These principles describe the direction of the project. They are design commitments to test and refine, not claims that a particular interface already achieves them.

## 1. Learning and useful work belong together

A project can have an **artifact goal**—such as a prototype, analysis, or decision—and a **learning goal**—such as understanding a concept or practicing a method. Both deserve attention, but neither has to be maximized on every task.

**In practice:** make both goals visible and allow the user to revise them. Avoid assuming that a polished artifact proves understanding.

## 2. The user directs the work

The user chooses goals, constraints, assistance, and consequential decisions. AI can suggest, challenge, and carry out agreed work, but should not quietly replace the user's judgment.

**In practice:** offer clear choices, explain meaningful tradeoffs, and let the user override or skip suggested steps.

## 3. Help should fit the moment

There is no universally correct amount of AI assistance. A question may help someone learn; a direct answer may be better when they are stuck, short on time, or working outside their learning goal.

**In practice:** support adjustable [assistance modes](assistance-modes.md). Never make the user earn a direct answer.

## 4. Understanding is not the same as fluency

Reading a persuasive explanation or approving generated work can feel like understanding without providing an opportunity to apply it.

**In practice:** occasionally offer a prediction, explanation, or application exercise when useful. Make it optional and avoid grading the person.

## 5. Evidence and uncertainty should be visible

Separate observations, sourced claims, assumptions, interpretations, and guesses. Confidence and model agreement do not establish truth.

**In practice:** preserve sources where available, identify missing evidence, and say when a claim has not been verified. Never invent citations.

## 6. Disagreement is useful information

Different perspectives can reveal blind spots and alternative approaches. A forced compromise can hide important conflicts.

**In practice:** preserve substantive disagreements, their reasons, and what evidence could resolve them. The user makes the final decision.

## 7. Reflection should help, not burden

Learning benefits from noticing what changed and what remains unclear, but constant interruption can undermine motivation and progress.

**In practice:** offer brief, skippable reflection at natural stopping points. Adapt its frequency to the user's preference.

## 8. Context belongs to the user

The project's goals, sources, decisions, and artifacts should not be trapped inside one conversation or tied unnecessarily to one model.

**In practice:** aim for inspectable, editable, exportable project records. Be transparent about what is stored or sent to model providers, and give users control over sensitive information.

## 9. Evaluate the experience, not just output speed

Useful results, user understanding, agency, evidence quality, and sustainable effort all matter. None has a perfect single metric.

**In practice:** use feedback and optional checks to improve the workflow, not to assign an authoritative score to a person's intelligence or worth.
